# How to get a literature review drafted in a weekend (without cutting corners)

*A realistic two-day plan, from a blank page to a draft you can actually defend.*

---

"Literature review due Monday" is a sentence that has ruined a lot of weekends. It doesn't have to. The reason reviews take forever usually isn't the writing. It's the searching, losing track of what you read, and staring at a pile of summaries that refuse to turn into an argument.

Here's a plan that fixes each of those, split across two days.

## Saturday morning: question and search (3 hours)

**Write your question in one sentence.** Not a topic, a question. "Does early mobilisation after hip surgery reduce length of stay in older adults?" is something you can search for and argue about. "Hip surgery recovery" isn't.

**Break it into two or three sub-questions.** These will become the sections of your review.

**Search more than one source.** Every database misses things. For health topics, use PubMed or Europe PMC plus one broad engine like Semantic Scholar or OpenAlex. For computer science, add arXiv. Try synonyms and both British and American spellings.

**Save generously.** Anything plausibly relevant goes in. You're filtering later.

*In DSCVR:* one search runs across arXiv, Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, Europe PMC, Crossref, DOAJ, PLOS and Zenodo at once. Create a category for the review and a topic for each sub-question, then save papers straight into them.

## Saturday afternoon: screen and read (4 hours)

**Screen titles and abstracts in batches of 20.** Include, exclude or maybe. Be strict with "include".

**Read the included papers with a template.** For each one, write down the question, the method, the main finding in one sentence, one limitation, and how it relates to papers you've already read. Same headings every time.

**Don't read every word.** Abstract, figures, introduction's last paragraph, results, discussion. Methods only when you need to judge quality.

*In DSCVR:* open each paper in the reader and ask Pinda to summarise it or list its limitations. Pinda reads the full PDF and answers from it. Save your template notes on the paper itself so they're there tomorrow.

## Sunday morning: themes and gaps (2 hours)

This is where a list becomes a review. Lay your notes side by side and ask:

- Which papers answer the same sub-question?
- Where do they agree? Where do they conflict?
- Do the conflicts line up with study design, population, setting or date?
- What has nobody looked at?

Write one sentence per paragraph that states a claim about the literature. "Single-centre studies reported large effects that multicentre trials did not reproduce" is a paragraph. "Smith (2019) found..." is not.

*In DSCVR:* make a Comparison report from the papers in a topic to see their aims, methods, results and limitations side by side. Then fact-check your boldest claim with Parliament before you build a paragraph on it.

## Sunday afternoon: draft and check (4 hours)

**Build a skeleton first:** question, sub-question headings, and your claim sentences. Then add evidence under each claim. Write the introduction and conclusion last.

**Cite as you go.** Fixing references at midnight is where mistakes happen.

**Check every claim against the paper.** Open the PDF and find the sentence. This step is non-negotiable, especially if AI helped anywhere.

*In DSCVR:* choose New report, pick Literature review, select your sources (up to 40), and let Pinda draft it with numbered citations. Use that draft to see the shape of the argument faster, then rewrite it in your own voice and check every citation. Export as Markdown, and copy citations in APA, Harvard, Vancouver or whatever your course uses.

## The honest part

A weekend is enough for a solid draft of a coursework review. It's not enough for a systematic review or a thesis chapter, which need a documented search strategy and far more screening. And no tool replaces the thinking in step three: seeing the pattern is your job.
