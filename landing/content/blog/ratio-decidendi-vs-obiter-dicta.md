Every law student meets the same problem early: a judgment runs to fifty pages, and somewhere in it is the part that binds future courts. That part is the ratio decidendi. Everything else the judge says is obiter dicta. Learning to tell them apart is one of the core skills of studying law.

## Definitions

**Ratio decidendi** (Latin for "the reason for the decision") is the legal principle the court needed to reach its decision on the material facts. It's the part of a judgment that binds lower courts under the doctrine of precedent.

**Obiter dicta** ("things said by the way") are everything else: comments on hypothetical facts, observations on points that didn't need deciding, and remarks about how the law might develop. Obiter can be persuasive, especially from senior courts, but it doesn't bind.

## Why it matters

When you cite a case in an essay or problem question, you need to say what it actually decided. Treating obiter as binding, or missing the ratio, is one of the most common ways to lose marks.

## How to find the ratio

### 1. Identify the material facts

Which facts did the court treat as important to the outcome? Facts the decision would survive without are probably not material.

### 2. Identify the question the court answered

Write the legal issue as a question, for example: "Does a manufacturer owe a duty of care to the ultimate consumer of its product?"

### 3. Find the answer and the reasoning necessary for it

The ratio is the principle that answers that question on those facts. Look for phrases like "the question is", "in my judgment" or "I would hold".

### 4. Use the reversal test

Take the proposition you think is the ratio and imagine the court had decided it the other way. If the outcome would change, it's likely part of the ratio. If not, it's likely obiter.

## When there are several judgments

In appellate courts with multiple judges, find the reasoning shared by the majority. If judges agreed on the outcome for different reasons, the ratio may be narrow, or arguably there may be no single ratio. Note concurring and dissenting judgments separately.

## Common signs of obiter

- "If it had been necessary to decide this point..."
- Discussion of facts that weren't before the court.
- Comments in a dissenting judgment.
- Broad statements wider than the facts needed.

## Check against later cases

Later courts often restate a case's ratio when they apply, distinguish or overrule it. Reading how later judgments describe a case is a good check on your own reading.
