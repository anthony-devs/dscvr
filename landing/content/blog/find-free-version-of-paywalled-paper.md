You click a paper you need and the publisher wants $40 to let you read it. Before you pay, or give up, know this: for a large share of papers, a free and completely legal copy already exists. It just isn't on the publisher's site.

This guide explains where those copies come from, how they differ from the published version, and how to find one quickly.

## The three versions of a paper

Most journal articles exist in up to three versions.

- **Preprint (submitted version).** The manuscript the authors sent to the journal, before peer review. Usually posted on a preprint server such as arXiv, bioRxiv, medRxiv, SSRN or a field-specific archive.
- **Accepted manuscript (author's accepted version, AAM, postprint).** The final text after peer review and revisions, accepted by the journal but not yet copy-edited or typeset. The content matches the published paper; the layout, page numbers and small edits may not.
- **Version of record (published version).** The typeset article on the publisher's website, with the journal's formatting and page numbers. This is usually the one behind the paywall.

## Why free versions exist

Many universities and research funders require their researchers to make their work free to read. Most journals allow authors to share the accepted manuscript in their university's repository or a subject archive, sometimes after an embargo of 6 to 24 months. This is often called **green open access**: the journal version stays paywalled, but a legal free copy sits in a repository.

So the paper you need may be free in an institutional repository, on PubMed Central, on arXiv, or on the author's own page.

## Can you cite an accepted manuscript?

Yes, with care:

- Cite the **published version** in your reference list (journal, volume, issue, pages and DOI), because that is the version of record.
- If you quote directly, check the page numbers against the published version, or quote by section instead. The text matches but the pagination won't.
- If you can only use a **preprint**, check whether a peer-reviewed version has since been published. Preprints can change a lot after review, and some never pass it. If you cite a preprint, say so (most styles add "Preprint" and the server name).

## How to find the free version

**1. Search the exact title.** Put the full title in quotation marks in Google Scholar and click "All versions". Repository copies often show up as a [PDF] link on the right.

**2. Look up the DOI in an open-access index.** Services built on the Unpaywall database take a DOI and return the best legal free copy, along with which version it is.

**3. Check the big free archives.** PubMed Central and Europe PMC for biomedicine, arXiv for physics, maths and computer science, SSRN for social science and law, CORE for repositories worldwide.

**4. Check the authors' university repository.** Search the university's repository for the title or the first author's name.

**5. Ask the author.** If nothing turns up, a short, polite email asking for a copy for your studies works more often than you would expect.

## A quick checklist

- Search the title in quotes and check "All versions".
- Look the DOI up in an open-access index.
- Note which version you found: preprint, accepted manuscript or published.
- Cite the published version, with its DOI.
- Check page numbers before quoting from an accepted manuscript.
- Ask the author if all else fails.
