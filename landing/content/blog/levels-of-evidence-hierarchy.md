The evidence hierarchy ranks study designs by how well they usually protect against bias when answering questions about treatment effects. It's often drawn as a pyramid, and it appears in almost every nursing, medicine and public health course.

## The pyramid, from top to bottom

1. **Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** of randomised controlled trials.
2. **Randomised controlled trials (RCTs).**
3. **Cohort studies.**
4. **Case-control studies.**
5. **Cross-sectional studies and case series.**
6. **Case reports.**
7. **Expert opinion, editorials and mechanistic reasoning.**

Different organisations number the levels differently, so always use the scheme your course or guideline uses.

## Why designs rank this way

- **Randomisation** balances known and unknown confounders between groups, which observational studies can't guarantee.
- **Prospective** designs measure exposure before outcomes, reducing recall bias.
- **Systematic reviews** combine many studies, which increases precision and shows whether results are consistent.

## The pyramid is a guide, not a rule

- **A badly run RCT** can be less trustworthy than a well-run cohort study.
- **The right design depends on the question.** Questions about prognosis or harm are often best answered by cohort studies. Experiences and meanings call for qualitative research, which the classic pyramid ignores.
- **A systematic review is only as good as its included studies.**

Frameworks like GRADE grade the overall certainty of evidence for an outcome, starting from the design and then moving up or down for things like risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision and large effects.

## Using levels of evidence in assignments

- State the design of each study you cite.
- Explain why the design matters for your question.
- Weigh conflicting findings by quality, not by how many studies say each thing.
- Note when the best available evidence is weak.
