Rereading feels productive. Highlighting feels productive. Neither works very well. Decades of research on learning point the same way: the most reliable way to remember something is to practise pulling it out of your memory. That's active recall.

## Why testing yourself works

When you reread, the material feels familiar, and familiarity is easy to mistake for knowing. When you test yourself, you find out what you actually know, and the effort of retrieving an answer strengthens the memory. Spacing those tests out over days and weeks makes the effect stronger still.

## How to write a good flashcard

- **One idea per card.** "What are the causes of hyponatraemia?" is a list, not a card. Split it.
- **Ask a question, don't show a term.** "Which nerve supplies the deltoid?" beats "Deltoid: ?".
- **Keep the answer short.** One to three sentences. If it's longer, the card is too big.
- **Use your own words** on the answer side, so you're not just memorising phrasing.

## How to write a good practice question

Single-best-answer questions, the format most professional exams use, test whether you can apply knowledge, not just recall it.

- Give a short scenario, then ask one clear question.
- Write four options that are all plausible to someone who half-knows the topic.
- Make one option clearly best for someone who fully knows it.
- Write a short explanation of why the answer is right and why each distractor is wrong. The explanation is where most of the learning happens.

## A simple weekly routine

1. After each lecture or reading, make 10 to 20 cards or questions.
2. Review new cards the next day, then three days later, then a week later.
3. Spend more time on the cards you get wrong. Mark the ones you know and move on.
4. Before an exam, do mixed practice questions across topics, not one topic at a time.
