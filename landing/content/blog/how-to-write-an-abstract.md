The abstract is the most-read part of any paper. Many readers will read nothing else, and it's what search engines and databases index. A good abstract tells them exactly what you did and found in a few hundred words.

## How long should an abstract be?

Usually **150 to 300 words**. Journals and conferences set their own limits, so check the guidelines first. Dissertation abstracts are often longer, up to 300 to 500 words.

## The standard structure

Most abstracts follow the shape of the paper, often with these headings in structured abstracts:

1. **Background:** why the question matters, in one or two sentences.
2. **Aim:** what the study set out to do.
3. **Methods:** design, participants or data, setting, key measures.
4. **Results:** the main findings, with key numbers.
5. **Conclusion:** what the findings mean and why they matter.

Unstructured abstracts use the same order without the headings.

## A fill-in template

> [Topic] is important because [reason]. However, [gap in what is known]. This study aimed to [aim]. We [design] with [participants or data] in [setting], measuring [main outcome]. We found that [main result, with numbers]. [Secondary result if essential.] These findings suggest [interpretation], which [implication for practice, policy or research].

## Tips

- **Write it last**, once you know what you found.
- **Lead with your contribution.** Don't spend half the word count on background.
- **Include real numbers** for the main result.
- **Use the words people search for** in your field, so the right readers find it.
- **Make it stand alone:** no undefined abbreviations, no citations, no references to figures.

## Common mistakes

- Background that never gets to the point.
- Methods so vague no one can tell what you did.
- Results described as "will be discussed".
- Conclusions stronger than the results support.
- Going over the word limit.
