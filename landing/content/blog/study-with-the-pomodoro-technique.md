The Pomodoro technique is simple: work on one thing for a set time, take a short break, repeat. It was named after a tomato-shaped kitchen timer. It works because it turns "study all afternoon" into a series of small, finishable commitments.

## The basic method

1. Pick one task. Not "revise cardiology", but "make flashcards for heart failure drugs".
2. Set a timer, traditionally for 25 minutes.
3. Work only on that task until the timer ends.
4. Take a five-minute break away from the screen.
5. After four blocks, take a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes.

## How long should a block be?

Twenty-five minutes is a starting point, not a rule. Short blocks of 15 minutes help on days when starting is the hard part. Longer blocks of 45 to 50 minutes suit deep work like writing or difficult reading, once you're warmed up. Experiment and keep what works.

## What to do in the breaks

Stand up, drink water, look at something far away. Avoid your phone if you can: a five-minute scroll rarely stays five minutes, and it fills your head with things that aren't your task.

## Make every session end with a plan

The most underrated part of a study session is the last two minutes. Before you stop, write down:

- What you covered.
- The two or three things you most need to remember.
- What to do first next time.

That note removes the "where was I?" problem and makes the next session start faster.

## Studying together

Working alongside other people, even silently, makes it easier to keep going. A shared timer means everyone breaks at the same time, so nobody interrupts anyone else.
