A literature review is not a list of summaries. It's an argument about what is known on a topic, where people disagree, and what is still missing. That's why it feels hard: you have to read a lot, then step back far enough to see the shape of the field.

The good news is that the process is the same every time. Here it is, step by step.

## 1. Narrow the question before you search

"Sepsis" is a topic. "Does the timing of the first antibiotic dose change mortality in adults with sepsis?" is a question. A review built on a question is easier to search for, easier to read for, and much easier to write.

Write your question in one sentence. Then write two or three sub-questions underneath it. These become the sections of your review later.

## 2. Search more than one database

No single database covers everything. Preprints live on arXiv and medRxiv. Biomedical papers are indexed in PubMed and Europe PMC. Open-access journals are listed in DOAJ. Citation-aware search engines like Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex cover almost every discipline.

A sensible search pass looks like this:

- Run your main keywords in two or three sources, not one.
- Add synonyms and spelling variants: "paediatric" and "pediatric", "randomised" and "randomized".
- Note how many results each source returns. If one returns thousands, your question is too broad.
- Save anything plausibly relevant. You'll filter later.

For each source you keep, record where it came from and how you found it. If your review needs a methods section, you'll need this.

## 3. Screen titles and abstracts quickly

Your first read is a filter, not a study session. For each paper, read the title and abstract and decide: include, exclude, or maybe. Be generous with "maybe" and strict with "include".

Work in batches of 20 to 30. Decision fatigue is real, and you'll make better calls in short bursts.

## 4. Read the papers you kept, with a purpose

For each included paper, you want the same handful of things:

- **The question** it asked.
- **The method**: study design, sample, setting.
- **The main finding**, in one sentence.
- **The limitations**, especially the ones the authors admit.
- **How it relates** to the other papers: does it agree, disagree, or extend?

Keep these in a consistent place. A spreadsheet works. A notes app works. What doesn't work is scattering them across PDF margins you'll never find again.

## 5. Group by theme, not by paper

This is the step that turns summaries into a review. Look across your notes and ask:

- Which papers answer the same sub-question?
- Where do findings agree, and where do they conflict?
- Do the conflicts line up with something, such as study design, population or date?
- What has nobody studied yet?

Each answer becomes a paragraph. A strong paragraph makes a claim about the literature, then uses several papers as evidence: "Early trials suggested a large benefit, but three later multicentre trials did not reproduce it."

## 6. Find the gap

The gap is the most important part of your review, because it justifies whatever comes next: your project, your recommendation, your thesis. Gaps usually look like one of these:

- A population that hasn't been studied.
- A method that hasn't been tried.
- Conflicting results that nobody has explained.
- An old consensus that newer evidence questions.

## 7. Write the review in layers

Start with a skeleton: your question, your sub-questions as headings, and one sentence per paragraph saying what the paragraph will claim. Then fill in the evidence. Write the introduction and conclusion last, once you know what you actually found.

Cite as you go. Rebuilding references at the end is where errors creep in.

## 8. Check every claim against the source

Before you submit, go back through each claim and confirm the paper says what you said it says. This matters even more if you used AI anywhere in the process. General chatbots are known to produce citations that look real but aren't. Whatever tool you use, the rule is the same: if you can't open the paper and find the sentence, don't cite it.
