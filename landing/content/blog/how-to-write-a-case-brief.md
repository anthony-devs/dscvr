A case brief is a one-page summary of a judgment that you can revise from, argue from and cite from. Writing good ones is one of the most useful habits in law school, because it forces you to find what the case actually decided.

## What goes into a case brief

Most briefs use the same headings:

- **Case name and citation.** Exactly as it's reported.
- **Court and date.** Which court decided it matters for precedent.
- **Judges.** Useful when there are dissents or notable concurring judgments.
- **Facts.** Only the material facts: the ones that affected the outcome.
- **Procedural history.** How the case got to this court.
- **Issues.** The legal questions the court had to answer, written as questions.
- **Holding.** The court's answer to each issue.
- **Ratio decidendi.** The legal principle necessary for the decision.
- **Obiter dicta.** Other legal observations that weren't necessary for the decision.
- **Dissenting or concurring opinions.**
- **Outcome.** The order: appeal allowed, dismissed, remitted.
- **Authorities.** Key statutes and cases relied on.

## Finding the ratio

The ratio is the hardest part and the most important. A practical test: take the proposition you think is the ratio and ask, "If the court had decided this point the other way, would the outcome have changed?" If yes, it's probably part of the ratio. If no, it's probably obiter.

Other clues:

- Look for where the judge says "the question is" or "in my judgment".
- In appellate cases with several judgments, find the reasoning a majority shares.
- Compare how later cases describe the decision. Courts often restate the ratio when they apply or distinguish it.

## Ratio versus obiter

Obiter dicta can still be persuasive, especially from senior courts, but they don't bind lower courts. When a judge says "if it had been necessary to decide this, I would have held...", that's obiter.
