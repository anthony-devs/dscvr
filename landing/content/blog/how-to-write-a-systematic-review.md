A systematic review answers a focused question by finding, appraising and combining all the relevant studies, using methods set out in advance so someone else could repeat them. That last part is what separates it from a literature review. Here is the process in plain terms.

## 1. Ask a focused question

Most systematic reviews start from a structured question, often in PICO form: population, intervention, comparison, outcome. A narrow question keeps the review manageable.

## 2. Write and register a protocol

The protocol sets out your question, eligibility criteria, search strategy, screening process and analysis plan before you start. Health-related reviews are often registered on PROSPERO, the international register run by the University of York. Registering helps avoid duplicate reviews and shows you didn't change your methods after seeing the results.

## 3. Build a search strategy

- Choose databases suited to your field: for health, typically MEDLINE (via PubMed), Embase, CINAHL and the Cochrane Library.
- Combine controlled vocabulary (like MeSH terms) with free-text keywords.
- Use OR within concepts and AND between them.
- Document the exact search for each database, with the date you ran it.
- Consider grey literature: trial registries, theses and reports.

A librarian can help a great deal at this stage.

## 4. Screen in two stages

First screen titles and abstracts, then the full texts of anything that might be eligible. Ideally two reviewers screen independently and resolve disagreements. Record how many studies you excluded at each stage, and why at the full-text stage.

## 5. Extract data

Use a standard form for every included study: design, setting, participants, intervention, comparison, outcomes, results and funding. Pilot the form on a few studies first.

## 6. Assess risk of bias

Use a recognised tool that fits the study designs, such as Cochrane's RoB 2 for randomised trials or ROBINS-I for non-randomised studies. Risk of bias affects how much weight each study's result deserves.

## 7. Synthesise

If studies are similar enough, a meta-analysis combines their results statistically. If not, a structured narrative synthesis is appropriate. Either way, explain differences between studies rather than averaging them away.

## 8. Report with PRISMA

The PRISMA 2020 statement sets out what to report, including a flow diagram showing records identified, screened, excluded and included. Most journals expect it.

## Common mistakes

- A question too broad to finish.
- Searching only one database.
- Changing inclusion criteria after seeing results.
- Skipping risk of bias assessment.
- Not recording reasons for exclusion.
