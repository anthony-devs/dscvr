Everyone starts with good intentions and ends up with a Downloads folder full of files called "1-s2.0-S0140673620..." The fix isn't a perfect tool. It's a small system you'll actually keep.

## Organize around questions, not sources

Folders called "PubMed" or "Week 3" don't help when you write. Organize by what you're trying to answer:

- One **category** per project, module or thesis chapter.
- One **topic** inside it for each sub-question or theme.

When it's time to write a section, everything for it is already in one place.

## Keep the reference and the PDF together

A paper you can't cite is useless, and a citation you can't open is risky. Whatever tool you use, keep the citation details, the PDF and your notes attached to each other.

## A naming rule for files

If you save PDFs as files, use one pattern and never deviate:

> FirstAuthor_Year_ShortTitle.pdf

For example: Rivers_2001_EarlyGoalDirectedTherapy.pdf. You can find anything by sorting or searching.

## Write a note the day you read it

Three lines are enough:

1. The main finding in one sentence.
2. The method in a line.
3. Why you saved it.

Without this, you'll re-read the paper later to remember why it mattered.

## Rate what you read

A simple 1-5 star rating saves hours later. When you're writing, sort by rating and start with the papers that mattered most.

## A weekly ten-minute tidy

Once a week:

- File anything sitting in "unsorted".
- Delete duplicates.
- Add notes to anything you read but didn't note.
- Move papers you didn't use into an "archive" topic.

## Reference managers

Tools like Zotero, Mendeley and EndNote are built for managing citations and inserting them into documents. If your course or lab uses one, use it, and export your library regularly.
