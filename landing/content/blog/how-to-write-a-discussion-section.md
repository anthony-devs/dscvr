The discussion is where you explain what your results mean. It's often the hardest section to write, because there's no data to report, only argument. A clear structure makes it much easier.

## The structure that works

Most strong discussions follow the same order:

1. **Restate the main finding** in one or two sentences, without numbers.
2. **Compare with previous research.** Where do your results agree, and where do they differ?
3. **Explain the differences.** Population, method, setting, timing, measurement.
4. **Implications.** What should researchers, practitioners or policymakers take from this?
5. **Strengths and limitations.** Honest and specific.
6. **Future research.** Questions your study raises but can't answer.
7. **Conclusion.** One short paragraph.

## 1. Lead with the answer

Open with what you found, in plain words: "Students who used spaced practice remembered more at four weeks than those who reread their notes." Don't repeat the results section. Interpret it.

## 2. Put it in context

This is where your literature review pays off. For each key finding, cite the studies that support it and the ones that don't. "This is consistent with..." and "In contrast to..." are your workhorse phrases.

## 3. Explain, don't just list

When your results differ from others', offer reasons. Different populations, different measures or a smaller sample are all legitimate explanations. Be clear when you're speculating.

## 4. Limitations: be specific

"The sample was small" is weak. "With 42 participants from one university, the study may not generalise to other settings, and it was underpowered to detect small effects" is useful. Say what each limitation means for how far the reader should trust your conclusion.

## 5. Don't overclaim

- Correlational data can't show causation.
- One study rarely "proves" anything.
- Avoid introducing new results that weren't in the results section.

## Useful phrases

- "These findings suggest that..."
- "One possible explanation is..."
- "This differs from [study], which may reflect..."
- "These results should be interpreted with caution because..."
- "Future studies could test whether..."
