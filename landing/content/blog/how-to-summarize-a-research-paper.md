A good summary of a research paper does one job: it lets you, or your reader, understand what the paper found and whether it matters, without reading the whole thing again. Most summaries fail because they copy the abstract or retell the paper in order. Here's a better way.

## What a research paper summary should include

Every useful summary answers six questions:

1. **What question did the authors ask?**
2. **Why does it matter?** One sentence of context.
3. **How did they answer it?** Study design, sample, setting, main measure.
4. **What did they find?** The main result, with the key number if there is one.
5. **What are the limitations?** Especially the ones that change how much you trust the result.
6. **What does it mean?** For your topic, your essay or practice.

If your summary answers those six questions, it's complete, however short it is.

## How long should a summary be?

- **For your own notes:** five to eight lines. Enough to remember the paper in six months.
- **For an annotated bibliography:** usually 150 to 250 words, check your course guide.
- **For a journal club or presentation:** one slide or half a page, plus the figure that carries the result.

## Step by step

### 1. Read for the argument first

Read the abstract, the last paragraph of the introduction, the figures and the conclusion. Write one sentence: "This paper shows that..." If you can't, read the results section before going further.

### 2. Pull out the facts

Fill in the six questions above in note form. Copy numbers exactly, and note the page or section where you found them.

### 3. Write it in your own words

Close the PDF and write the summary from your notes. This is the step that stops accidental plagiarism, and it's also where you find out what you didn't understand.

### 4. Check it against the paper

Reopen the paper and confirm every number and claim. Remove anything the paper doesn't actually say.

## A summary template you can copy

> **Citation:** Author(s), year, title, journal.
> **Question:** What the authors set out to answer.
> **Design and sample:** Type of study, who or what was studied, how many.
> **Main finding:** One or two sentences, with the key result.
> **Limitations:** The one or two that matter most.
> **Relevance:** How this connects to your question.

## Common mistakes

- **Copying the abstract.** It's the authors' framing, not your understanding.
- **Listing every result.** Pick the main one. Secondary findings go in only if they matter to you.
- **Leaving out limitations.** A summary without limitations makes weak studies look strong.
- **Adding opinions as facts.** Keep "the authors found" separate from "I think".
