Most people read papers the way they read novels: first word to last word. That's the slowest possible way to read research, and often the least useful. Papers are built to be read out of order.

Here's a method that works for almost any field.

## Pass 1: five minutes to decide if it matters

Read only these parts:

- **Title and abstract.** What question, what method, what answer?
- **Figures and tables.** In many papers, the main result is one figure.
- **The last paragraph of the introduction.** Authors usually state their contribution there in plain words.
- **The conclusion.**

Then ask: is this relevant to what I'm doing? If not, stop. You've lost five minutes, not an hour.

## Pass 2: thirty minutes to understand the argument

Now read the introduction, results and discussion properly, but skim the methods. Your goals:

- Understand the claim and the evidence for it.
- Mark anything you don't understand rather than getting stuck on it.
- Note the key references you might need to read next.

By the end of pass 2 you should be able to explain the paper to a friend in three sentences. Try it out loud.

## Pass 3: only when you need the details

Read the methods carefully when you need to judge the paper's quality, reproduce it, or cite a specific detail. Ask:

- Was the design right for the question? A cohort study can't prove causation the way a randomised trial can.
- How big was the sample, and who was in it?
- What did they measure, and when?
- What do the authors admit as limitations, and what did they not mention?

Most papers you read for coursework only need passes 1 and 2.

## Take notes that survive

The note you write now is the one you'll use in three weeks when you're writing. Keep it short and consistent:

1. One-sentence summary of the finding.
2. The method in a line.
3. One strength and one limitation.
4. How it connects to other papers you've read.

Store it next to the paper, not in a separate notebook you'll lose.

## When you get stuck on a section

Jargon, statistics and dense methods sections slow everyone down. Two habits help:

- Look up terms as you go, but write the definition in your own words.
- Ask a specific question rather than "what does this mean?": "Why did they adjust for age here?" is answerable; "explain the methods" usually isn't.
